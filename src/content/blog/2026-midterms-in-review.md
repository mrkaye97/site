Yesterday, I wrote [a post](/blog/2026-midterms) saying that I thought there would be a blue wave. It was more like a Blue Mega Tsunami. Things went basically as well for the Democrats as they could've. As of now (around 11\:30PM Eastern), it looks like a wave election like we haven't seen in 40 years or so.

There's tons of election content so I won't write too much on it, but I thought I'd write down a few bullets from some numbers that I thought were the craziest of the night:

- _**One**_ hour: Roughly how long after polls started closing for the betting markets to show a lead for Democrats that seemed decisive enough to make people following the election think "this is over."
- _**Eight**_: The number of seats the Democrats are going to flip in the Senate.
- _**Eighteen**_ points: Jon Ossoff's lead in Georgia, an R+2 state in 2024, with 95% of votes reported.
- _**Six**_: The number of consecutive elections won by Gina Hinojosa in Texas.
- _**Twenty-five**_ points: The difference between the percentage chance of winning for the Democrats according to the New York Times's "Needle" and the percentage chance given by Kalshi at 8PM Eastern.
- _**Twenty-three**_ points: The swing from Trump's 2024 margin of victory in Kansas to Adam Hamilton's lead with 95% reporting.
- _**Fifty-six**_ points: Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez's lead in her district (NY 14) with 95% reporting.

There were a few clear winners and losers from the night:

### Winners

- _**"Non-traditional" Democrats**_ like Adam Hamilton, James Talarico, Mary Peltola, etc. Candidates who might've been labeled something like "Pro-Gun Democrats" or "Pro-Life Democrats" or some such a few years ago: The ones who don't align with the party on every major issue, but are better fits for their states as a result. You might also call these "Manchin Democrats" at risk of being chastised.
- _**Jon Ossoff**_, with no real explanation needed.
- _**Silver Bulletin, The Argument, and "data journalism" in general.**_ These outlets have been saying we were in Blue Wave territory for _months_, and Silver Bulletin's polling average showed a D+9 generic ballot, and The Argument's final poll was D+11. People just didn't want to believe it.
- _**Candidate quality**_: If this election's results weren't a referendum on candidate quality being important, I don't know what would be. Races with Republicans running "good" candidates (e.g. Susan Collins in Maine) performed better than races with Republicans running "bad" candidates (e.g. Paxton).

### Losers

- _**MAGA and Trumpism.**_ Ken Paxton, Vivek Ramaswamy, Mike Rogers, Darline Graham, Roger Marshall, and others got clobbered in recently deep red states.
- _**The "Expert Raters"**_ like Sabato's Crystal Ball and the Cook Political Report, who were hedging hard against calling this a blue wave environment until basically the day before the election.
- _**Progressive candidates in blue states**_: This was yet _another_ election that seemed to push back on the argument that progressives would perform surprisingly well in a general election (read: "Bernie would've beaten Trump in 2016"). For instance, Abdul El-Sayed ended up winning Michigan by about six points, and was outrun by Jocelyn Benson there by almost five (she won by almost 11 points).

And a couple of questions I have coming out of this:

1. Is this type of scathing review of Trump's second term enough for Republicans in Congress, especially ones who are concerned about their own re-election prospects in 2028, to start breaking with Trump in a way they haven't before?
2. After Ossoff's massive win in Georgia, is there anyone else really in contention to lead the Democratic ticket in 2028?
3. How much did candidate quality matter today? For instance, would Cornyn have beaten Talarico in Texas? Or would a non-Collins candidate in Maine have lost by significantly more?
