When I started my second job six years ago (at CollegeVine, as a data scientist), my then-manager-now-CTO asked me where I saw my career in five years. I told him I didn't know: It felt so far away.

At the time, I had about six months of work experience, and was feeling a combination of nervous from imposter syndrome, and thinking I had it all figured out. I had a philosophy that revolved around doing things the "right" way << filler >>. Now, I'd sum my engineering philosophy up pretty simply: Be practical, ship quickly. These, in my view, are the two most important things we can do and / or be as engineers to be effective.

> Note that this is likely true in other fields too (possibly outside of tech, definitely within tech), but I don't want to overstep. See note on "be practical."

Over time, I've become more and more aware that much of what I learned in college and from perusing the internet about working in tech is not always great advice. my experience has largely been that there are two major footguns that prevent people in roles like mine from being effective, which are basically the opposite of the two principles I try to follow. First, being dogmatic. And second, waiting too long for something to be "just right."

## Dogmatism

I can't count the number of times I've heard arguments in favor of approaching a problem a certain way that basically boil down to "that's just how you're supposed to do it." This often stems from good intentions: Building systems that scale, not accumulating technical debt, running A/B tests so we know with some degree of confidence which version of our button performs better, and so on.

## Janky vs. Polished

Over the next few months, I settled in what I thought was pretty well. I started working on some projects, which I thought were going pretty well, and as the first data science hire on the team, I took it to be my responsibility to spread the gospel on things like how to run A/B tests properly, and so on.

In hindsight, I cringe at these moments.
