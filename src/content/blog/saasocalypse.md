People keep talking about how AI is going to kill SaaS. And three seconds before writing this sentence, I had to pause Copilot Autocomplete in my IDE just to get the space think for myself instead of incessantly having our AI Overlords write my sentences for me. Anyways, there's a [whole website](https://saasocalypse.com/) to track the death of SaaS, [HN threads about it](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46911267), and so on.

The idea is that AI is going to kill SaaS because it makes it easier for people to build the SaaS tools they need, rather than buying them from a vendor. I'm praying, truly on my hands and knees, that this has the second-order effect of more companies getting back to one of YC's core pieces of advice: "Build something people want." My view is that people aren't migrating away from SaaS because it's just so easy to build everything yourself now. They're migrating away from SaaS because SaaS products have gotten bad (insufferable, even), and people are starting to wake up to the fact that it's easier than ever to build something that works for them as opposed to suffering and shouting complaints into a void every day.

Admittedly, I work for a B2B SaaS (developer tools) startup, so maybe I have a bias. But I also have something of a reputation for _hating_ working with common SaaS tools that people rely on often, both in their personal lives and at work. Without naming names, some of the ones that frustrate me the most are:

1. A Popular Project Management Tool (for moving tickets along and tracking work and such) that has so many features it's impossible to figure out how to apply a tag to an issue.
2. A Popular Incident Management Tool (for paging you when your infrastructure is on fire) which reminds you to write an incident summary every five seconds until you decide you can't take it anymore and smash your computer screen.
3. A Popular Platform for Storing and Managing Code, which has seemingly grown to include AI copilots, CI runners, product management tools, and downtime every day.

And so on. Again, not naming names.

The main issue I have with all of these tools, as well as lots of other software I use (or have tried to use, before the nausea set in), is that _they do too much_. Or they try to. I tend to find myself thinking often that I just want a tool that does something dead simple. For instance, for managing work, the "product" I want is a Kanban board on the wall of our office with sticky notes on it in a couple of columns (to do, doing, done). Unfortunately, we work remote. Not that it's unfortunate to work remote - that's quite fortunate. But working remote means we need to use _software_ for our Kanban board, and this is where things get hairy.

The main players in the space all basically are some flavor of The Product Which Must Not Be Named from the list above. And there's some notoriety about project management tools in tech broadly: Namely that everyone hates them all. But we're stuck, and we face a choice: Adopt one, or build something ourselves.

And this decision is, in a nutshell, the SaaS-ocalypse. AI has made it easier to build instead of buy, more teams build, customers churn, valuations evaporate. Likely SaaS companies have gone the product creep route because some CEO type or board thinks that they can earn more money with more features.

The irony, though, is that maybe it's the case that people actually just want the Kanban board on the wall, and would gladly pay for it instead of needing to build it themselves. But they don't want all of the additional bloat that comes with it that makes it hard for the tool to do what it says on the tin. Put another way: Sometimes people want a hammer, and they don't feel like their hammer also needs to include a screwdriver, a bottle opener, a power saw, and an AI slop customer support experience to frustrate them to their wit's end when they can't figure out how to use the Franken-tool anymore.
